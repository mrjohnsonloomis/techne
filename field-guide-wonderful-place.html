Human AI Lab · Session II

Field Guide to a Wonderful Place

In 1974, Ted Nelson argued against the kind of computer teaching everyone was about to build, and proposed something else instead. Today we build the something else.

Tuesday · 2:00–3:30 Nelson, Computer Lib / Dream Machines (1974)
Where We Are

From Algorithm to Argument

This morning's reading gave us the algorithm: al-Khwarizmi's procedure, the fixed sequence of steps. Nelson's target, in 1974, was that same logic applied to teaching: a system that breaks a subject into items, sequences them, and dresses the result up as conversation. He called it unnecessary, unscientific, and quietly cruel, and then proposed something else. Fifty years later, we have machines that genuinely talk back. This session asks what Nelson's alternative looks like now, and tries to build a piece of it.

  • Read (15 min): four short passages from Nelson, read aloud.
  • Ask (10 min): one question, before we touch anything.
  • Build (50 min): pick something you teach and build a place for it.
  • Share (15 min): one thing each.

Before We Build

The Question

Fifty years on, is a chatbot tutor the Now-Johnny machine Nelson was attacking, or the wonderful place he was imagining? What's the actual difference?

Prompting

Notes on Prompting

A few things that might help as you describe a place rather than hand over a spec.

  • Describe what you want the student to be able to do, see, and explore. The model can handle the code.
  • Get the overall shape working first, then add one feature at a time.
  • Look at what you get, describe in plain language what's wrong or missing, and ask for that change.
  • If the model suggests something you didn't ask for, try it before deciding whether to keep it.
  • Nelson wanted these things designed and edited by people, not programmed. That's still your job. The model is just doing the typing.
Same Subject, Two Prompts

Item & Sequence, or Wonderful Place

The difference often shows up in the prompt itself, before any code is written.

Item & Sequence Wonderful Place
Build a quiz on the French Revolution. Ask ten multiple-choice questions about key dates, figures, and causes. Track the student's score, and tell them after each question whether they got it right.
